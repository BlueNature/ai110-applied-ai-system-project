Guess-the-number games are a classic example of a search problem. One player secretly chooses a number from a known range, such as 1 to 100, while the other player makes guesses. After each guess, the player is told whether the secret number is higher, lower, or correct. The objective is to identify the number in as few guesses as possible. Although the game appears simple, it demonstrates an important principle from mathematics and computer science: every clue provides information that can be used to eliminate possibilities.

Many people naturally use strategies that feel reasonable but are not optimal. One common approach is to guess numbers in order, such as 1, 2, 3, 4, and so on. This guarantees success eventually but can require many guesses. Another common strategy is making random guesses within the remaining range. While randomness may occasionally get lucky, it does not consistently reduce the search space efficiently. Some players also favor "round numbers" like 25, 50, or 75 without adjusting their approach systematically after each clue. These methods often waste opportunities to eliminate large portions of the remaining possibilities.

The best strategy is called **binary search**. The idea is simple: always guess the middle number of the remaining possible range. If the answer is "higher," eliminate the entire lower half. If the answer is "lower," eliminate the entire upper half. Then repeat the process by guessing the middle of the new, smaller range. Each guess cuts the number of remaining possibilities roughly in half, making this strategy far more efficient than guessing sequentially or randomly.

For example, suppose the secret number is between 1 and 100. The first guess should be 50. If the clue is "higher," the remaining range becomes 51 to 100, so the next guess is 75. If the clue is "lower," the remaining range becomes 1 to 49, so the next guess is 25. This process continues, repeatedly dividing the remaining possibilities in half until only one number remains.

Binary search works because every high or low clue provides the maximum possible amount of useful information when the guess is in the middle of the remaining range. Guessing near one end of the range only eliminates a small number of possibilities if the clue points toward the larger section. By always choosing the midpoint, neither possible outcome leaves you with significantly more work than the other. This minimizes the maximum number of guesses needed regardless of which number was chosen.

The efficiency of binary search grows dramatically as the range becomes larger. A sequential search of 1 to 100 could require up to 100 guesses in the worst case, while binary search guarantees finding the number in at most 7 guesses. For a range of 1 to 1,000, binary search requires at most 10 guesses, and for 1 to 1,000,000, only about 20 guesses are needed. This logarithmic growth is why binary search is widely used in computing whenever information can be repeatedly divided into two parts.

To consistently perform well, keep track of the current lowest and highest possible numbers after every clue, guess the midpoint of that range, and immediately update the boundaries based on whether the answer is higher or lower. Avoid repeating guesses or making guesses outside the remaining possible range. By following this method, you always make the most informative guess available.

In summary, while many players rely on sequential, random, or intuition-based guessing, the optimal strategy is binary search. It succeeds because it systematically halves the remaining search space with every guess, ensuring the smallest possible worst-case number of guesses. This combination of simplicity, reliability, and mathematical efficiency makes binary search the best way to win high/low number guessing games.
